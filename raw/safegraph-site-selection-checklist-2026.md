# Retail Site Selection Checklist: 10 Steps for Choosing a Winning Location in 2026

Source: https://www.safegraph.com/blog/retail-site-selection-checklist/

**Author:** Briana Brown
**Published:** June 17, 2021 — **Updated:** August 12, 2026
**Category:** Industry Use Cases

## Main argument

Retail expansion in 2026 requires fewer, better-supported openings because shopping-center vacancy, construction expenses, lease costs, and store closures make poor location decisions expensive. The article recommends combining internal performance records with demographic, mobility, competitive, physical, financial, legal, and post-opening data. AI can accelerate screening, but human review and physical site inspections remain necessary.

Cited market context: U.S. shopping-center vacancy was 5.9% in Q1 2026; Coresight Research projected ~5,500 openings and ~7,900 closures in the U.S. in 2026; e-commerce was 16.9% of U.S. retail sales in Q1 2026; 88% of commercial real-estate investors had begun experimenting with AI, while only 5% had fully met their AI-program objectives.

## Ten-step retail site-selection framework

### 1. Map the existing store network
Plot current locations alongside sales, lease-expiration dates, and trade-area boundaries. Look for underserved areas, overlapping customer territories, store clusters with different results, and trade areas based on drive times/catchment modeling rather than simple distance.

### 2. Measure the effect of surrounding businesses
Map competitors, anchors, and complementary businesses around candidate sites and successful stores. Distinguish competitor clustering that increases comparison-shopping traffic, complementary co-tenants, anchors that raise visits, and nearby formats that suppress sales or intensify cannibalization.

### 3. Match locations to the actual customer profile
Overlay demographic variables (age, income, household structure, education) with the existing store base, then use mobility/visitation data to see where current customers originate. Profile high-performing stores' catchments and compare candidates to those profiles — population totals alone are insufficient.

### 4. Add physical and contextual site information
Review road visibility/sightlines, parking capacity/circulation/turnover, entry/exit arrangements, traffic signals, transit/highway access, and directional traffic by commute period. Importance varies by format: grocery stores need usable parking and circulation specifically.

### 5. Test financial and legal feasibility
Model full occupancy/opening cost: base rent, CAM/taxes/insurance, construction/TI expense, permits, zoning. Cited figures: average U.S. retail asking rent $24.69/sq ft/year (range ~$15/sq ft Phoenix to $85+/sq ft Manhattan); CAM/tax/insurance ~$2-4/sq ft; fit-out costs ~$40-300/sq ft (national average ~$149, SF example $228). Zoning/licensing checks should happen before lease commitment.

### 6. Analyze current-store performance
Rank existing stores using more than sales: revenue, visits/foot traffic, visit frequency, dwell time, customer origin, sales per square foot, seasonality, nearby competitor openings/closures — to separate durable performance from temporary/favorable circumstances.

### 7. Use lookalike and AI-assisted scoring
Build a model from best-performing locations' characteristics and apply it to new candidates, to screen large candidate lists, rank sites, find emerging trade areas, and estimate revenue. Presented as a speed/consistency tool, not a replacement for judgment.

### 8. Define the appropriate store footprint
Compare candidate spaces with successful stores' size/configuration: sales per sq ft, circulation, selling area, back-of-house needs, ceiling height, column locations, storefront width. A desirable trade area doesn't compensate for a badly-sized or awkward format.

### 9. Weight criteria by retail vertical
**Grocery-specific guidance:** use compact residential trade areas; emphasize parking, circulation, basket-oriented access, and competitive saturation. (QSR and apparel/mall retail have their own, different priority sets.)

### 10. Validate the forecast after opening
Review each new store at ~90 days and again at 12 months: compare forecast vs. actual visits, visit frequency, revenue, trade-area capture, customer origins, and performance by demographic segment. Update future scoring if customers come from unexpected areas or a store succeeds/fails for unmodeled reasons.

## Data sources and analytical methods

Recommends combining: internal sales/lease/format/performance records; Census and demographic data; mobility/visitation/dwell-time/customer-origin data; POI data for competitors/anchors/complementary businesses; drive-time/walk-time/catchment models; traffic and access observations; parking/site-layout information; street-level imagery and in-person inspection; rent/CAM/tax/insurance/construction/permitting data; zoning and licensing records; AI or statistical lookalike-scoring tools. Cites SafeGraph Places, Census Bureau e-commerce data, Coresight Research, Cushman & Wakefield, TenantBase, Terrapin Construction Group, GrowthFactor.ai, and Capital One Shopping Research.

## Additional FAQ points

- Trade-area analysis can use fixed radii, drive times, or observed visitation patterns — the last is said to better reflect actual customer behavior.
- Site-selection software pricing ranges from ~$200/month (self-service) to $100,000+/year (enterprise GIS/forecasting), with mid-market platforms often in the low five figures/year.
- Successful site selection should identify both expansion opportunities and underperforming/cannibalizing locations.
- A new store should be judged against its original forecast, not merely whether it reaches a flat revenue target.
